NIGERIAN STAPLE CROP CALENDAR AND GROWING CONDITIONS

This document covers five of Nigeria's most widely grown food crops - maize, cassava, yam, tomato, and fluted pumpkin (ugwu) - to ground crop-stress and planting-timing advice for smallholder farmers in a Nigerian context, complementing the general soil moisture guidance elsewhere in this corpus.

MAIZE: Thrives in Nigeria's rainy season, with planting typically starting between March and June depending on region (Southern Nigeria plants as early as March; Northern Nigeria typically waits for rains onset in May-June). Requires well-drained, loamy soil with pH 5.5-7.5. Harvest occurs 3-4 months after planting. Maize is highly sensitive to moisture stress in the first few weeks after planting - drought during germination and early growth significantly reduces yield.

CASSAVA: A staple crop that thrives in both wet and dry conditions but performs best during the rainy season, typically planted between April and June. Requires fertile, well-drained soil to support tuber development. Cassava has slow initial growth and is very susceptible to weed interference during the first 3-4 months after planting - uncontrolled weeds during this critical period can reduce yield substantially, so this window is a key vulnerability period distinct from moisture stress alone. Cassava is often intercropped with yam, maize, melon, cowpea, and soybean.

YAM: Requires high moisture content for germination, making it a rainy-season crop. Planting starts between March and May (Southern Nigeria plants earlier than the North), with harvest occurring 6-8 months after planting. Requires deep, loose soil rich in organic matter. Because yam has a long growth cycle spanning both the vulnerable planting window and the harvest-season period flagged elsewhere in this corpus as an elevated farmer-herder conflict risk window, standing yam crops face compounded exposure across the full farming season.

TOMATO: Unlike the other crops in this document, tomato is primarily a DRY SEASON crop in Nigeria (November-March), relying on irrigation rather than rainfall. This makes tomato farms particularly dependent on reliable water access (tank/well/irrigation monitoring) rather than rainfall timing, and is why tomato is grouped with dry-season vegetable production alongside pepper, onion, and cabbage.

FLUTED PUMPKIN (UGWU): One of Nigeria's most widely consumed vegetables, with an estimated over 40 million people consuming ugwu leaves daily. Commonly grown as a dry-season crop, propagated from seed, with recommended close spacing of approximately 50cm x 30cm for high-density planting. Responds well to organic fertilization (poultry manure pre-plant treatment is a common practice) followed by top-dressing with NPK fertilizer. As a leafy vegetable, ugwu is more sensitive to short-term water stress than root/tuber crops like cassava or yam, since leaf turgor drops quickly under moisture deficit.

GENERAL SEASONAL PATTERN: Nigeria's rainy season (roughly April-October) is the primary planting window for maize, rice, yam, cassava, and most staple field crops. The dry season (November-March) relies on irrigation and is used for vegetables such as tomato, ugwu, pepper, onion, and cabbage. This seasonal split is directly relevant when assessing crop vulnerability alongside the livestock-conflict seasonal risk patterns described elsewhere in this corpus - a farm growing dry-season vegetables faces a different water-dependency risk profile than one growing rain-fed staples.
