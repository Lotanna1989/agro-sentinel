NIGERIAN LIVESTOCK MARKET ADVISORY AND VERIFIED DISEASE SIGNS (CATTLE, RAM, GOAT)

This document provides reference price ranges and festival/seasonal timing for cattle, rams, and goats in Nigeria, combined with verified veterinary disease signs, to support a combined health+market sale advisory. Prices are approximate reference ranges from Nigerian livestock market reporting, not live real-time data - actual prices vary by market, negotiation, and current conditions. This system does not replace direct market inquiry or veterinary assessment before a sale.

CATTLE REFERENCE PRICE RANGES (whole-animal, outside festival demand spikes):
- 200-250 kg (medium/standard cow): approximately N600,000 - N950,000
- 300-350 kg (large cow): approximately N900,000 - N1,500,000
- 400-550 kg (giant/premium cow): approximately N1,300,000 - N2,100,000
Approximate derived per-kilogram baseline: roughly N2500 - N4000/kg live weight (planning estimate only, confirm at target market).

FESTIVAL/SEASONAL DEMAND - CRITICAL PRICING FACTOR: livestock prices in Nigeria are strongly tied to the Islamic calendar, not just dry/wet season. Eid-el-Kabir (Sallah/Eid-al-Adha) is the single largest demand spike of the year for rams, goats, and cows, since Muslim families traditionally sacrifice an animal for the celebration. In the run-up to Eid-el-Kabir 2026 (held May 27-28, 2026), documented market reporting showed extreme price surges: medium rams that sold for N130,000-N450,000 the prior year rose to N250,000-N800,000+ approaching the festival; premium/large rams reached N1.2 million to N1.5 million in some markets (Kano, Abuja, Lagos); large cows that previously sold around N900,000 rose to N1.5-N1.7 million in some eastern markets. Prices are reported to ease somewhat immediately after the festival as demand drops. Ramadan itself (the fasting month preceding Eid-el-Fitr) typically sees increased demand for smaller livestock and poultry for iftar/breaking-fast meals, though the price effect is generally smaller than the Eid-el-Kabir ram/cow surge. Eid-el-Fitr and Eid-el-Kabir dates shift each year on the Gregorian calendar (following the lunar Islamic calendar), so current festival timing should be checked rather than assumed from a fixed date.

RAM AND GOAT MARKET NOTES: rams (not goats) are the primary Eid-el-Kabir sacrificial animal in most Nigerian tradition, commanding the highest festival premiums of any livestock category relative to their non-festival baseline price. Goats see a smaller but still real demand increase around the same period, particularly among families substituting a smaller/cheaper animal amid rising ram costs - market reporting has specifically noted buyers shifting toward smaller animals when ram prices become unaffordable. Both ram and goat prices are also driven upward by general food inflation, transport cost, and regional insecurity affecting supply routes, similar to cattle.

OTHER PRICE-MOVING FACTORS (all species): general Nigerian inflation (headline inflation was reported around 15-16% in early 2026, pushing baseline livestock costs up year-on-year regardless of season); regional insecurity and transport route disruption reducing supply reaching major markets; breed and body condition, with well-conditioned animals commanding the higher end of their weight-class range and visibly unwell or poorly-conditioned animals selling below baseline.

VERIFIED CATTLE DISEASE SIGNS (for health-status assessment before a sale decision, based on veterinary/WOAH reference sources and Nigeria-specific research):

Foot-and-Mouth Disease (FMD) in ram, goat, or cattle: fever plus sores or blisters around the mouth, tongue, or hooves are the classic warning signs of FMD, a highly contagious disease confirmed present in Nigeria (documented cases include Bauchi State cattle). A ram, goat, or cow showing fever with mouth sores should NOT be sold or taken to market, even close to Sallah - selling a sick animal risks spreading disease to other animals at market and will fetch a lower price than a healthy one. Isolate the animal and contact veterinary services before considering any sale.

Lumpy Skin Disease (LSD) in cattle, sheeppox/goatpox in sheep and goats: firm skin nodules or lumps, fever, reduced appetite, and weight loss despite eating are the signs, documented among farmers in Northeast Nigeria. As with FMD, do not sell or transport an animal showing these signs until assessed.

Trypanosomiasis (tsetse-transmitted, grazing cattle): unlike the sudden-onset signs of FMD and LSD, this shows as a gradual pattern - progressive weight loss, pale gums, and lethargy over time despite the animal still eating normally.

HEALTH-TO-MARKET DECISION LOGIC: an animal showing any of the verified disease signs above (fever, visible sores/nodules, lameness, significant appetite loss, progressive weight loss) should generally NOT be sold or transported until assessed and the concern resolved - selling a visibly unwell animal both risks disease spread to the buyer's herd/market and reliably fetches a below-baseline price, making it a poor outcome even from a pure sale-value perspective. Mild, short-term appetite reduction without other symptoms (no fever, no visible lesions, animal otherwise alert and mobile) is less specific and may not require postponing a sale, but should still be weighed against the animal's overall condition. For heat-stress-related appetite reduction specifically (as opposed to infectious disease), see livestock_heat_stress.txt elsewhere in this corpus.

HOW THIS SHOULD BE USED IN AN ADVISORY RESPONSE: price estimation should be a deterministic calculation (weight multiplied by the appropriate per-kg baseline, adjusted for festival timing and known modifiers), not a number the language model invents. The recommended pattern: application-layer calculation produces the estimate first, then the language model explains and contextualizes that number alongside verified health signs and festival/seasonal timing - the model's role is synthesis and explanation, not acting as the live source of price or diagnosis.
